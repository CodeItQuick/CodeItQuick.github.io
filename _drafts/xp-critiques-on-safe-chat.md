## ChatGPT

Beyond Ron Jeffries, these are the **five strongest critiques from influential Agile, Scrum, product, and complexity thinkers**. They overlap, but each attacks SAFe from a different direction.

## 1. Ken Schwaber — *“unSAFe at any speed”*

Schwaber, Scrum’s co-creator, gives perhaps the most historically important early critique. He argues that SAFe resembles a reborn Rational Unified Process: a comprehensive, commercially packaged methodology that prioritizes prescribed processes and tools over the judgment of the people doing the work. His most useful distinction is:

> Values and principles can scale; practices are context-sensitive.

He recommends judging SAFe by measurable results such as cycle time, release frequency, defects, stabilization time, and employee and customer satisfaction—not by whether the organization successfully installed all the roles and ceremonies. citeturn830603view0turn847155search0

**Why read it:** It provides the clearest Manifesto-level argument against standardized enterprise Agile.

**Weakness:** It is short, combative, and does not deeply analyze every SAFe mechanism.

---

## 2. Marty Cagan — *“Revenge of the PMO”*

This is probably the **best organizational and product-development critique**. Cagan argues that SAFe allows the traditional project-management organization to return under Agile terminology. In his analysis, the program becomes the dominant unit, while teams are reduced to delivery groups implementing decisions made by product managers, architects, and release-train management.

His central criticisms are that SAFe:

- restores top-down program management;
- emphasizes output rather than outcomes;
- weakens empowered, durable product teams;
- separates decision-makers from engineers and designers;
- preserves the project mindset while adopting Agile vocabulary.

Cagan acknowledges that his evidence was largely second-hand, but his structural argument is detailed and highly relevant to the distinction between feature teams and empowered product teams. citeturn830603view3turn847155search3

**Why read it:** It most directly supports an argument that SAFe manages dependencies and delivery rather than restructuring the organization around products.

---

## 3. Jeff Gothelf — *“SAFe is Not Agile”*

Gothelf, co-author of *Lean UX* and *Sense & Respond*, attacks SAFe from the perspective of **discovery, experimentation, and customer learning**. He argues that SAFe optimizes for predictable execution of planned features, while real product development requires discoveries that invalidate plans and force course corrections.

His strongest observation is that continuous discovery is not merely an extra activity that can be inserted into a release train. Discovery will regularly change priorities, alter scope, and potentially invalidate the train’s plan. A system designed to protect predictability therefore tends to suppress the learning that should change the plan. citeturn830603view2turn847155search2

**Why read it:** It is the best complement to Jeffries’ delivery-focused critique because it explains why SAFe can deliver efficiently while still building the wrong things.

---

## 4. Dave Snowden — *“SAFe: the infantilism of management”*

Snowden’s critique comes from complexity science and organizational sense-making. He describes SAFe as a linear, manufacturing-style model imposed on work that behaves more like a changing ecology.

His central argument is not merely that SAFe contains too many processes. It is that SAFe gives management a way to **avoid genuine organizational change**. Instead of changing authority structures and learning to manage uncertainty, leaders can install a detailed framework that makes the existing hierarchy appear Agile.

He compares its dependency mapping and planning structures to renamed versions of older project-management techniques and argues that software development should be treated as a service and ecology rather than a predictable production line. citeturn830603view1

**Why read it:** It gives the strongest theoretical explanation for why SAFe’s planning model is inappropriate for complex product development.

**Weakness:** It is extremely polemical and less methodical than Cagan or Gothelf.

---

## 5. Mike Beedle — *“SAFe is NOT Agile. SAFe is not Even Scrum”*

Beedle was an Agile Manifesto co-author and an early Scrum author. His critique evaluates SAFe against all four Manifesto values rather than concentrating only on bureaucracy.

He argues that SAFe:

- places a massive process and hierarchy ahead of interactions;
- does not require sufficiently continuous integration and testing;
- creates long feedback intervals around release trains;
- provides too few mechanisms for adapting when business, architecture, technology, or teams change during the train;
- treats one release-train structure as broadly applicable despite differing contexts.

The currently accessible version is an **authorized archival republication**: the original English post appeared in a Scrum Alliance LinkedIn group, but that original is no longer available. citeturn813896view0

**Why read it:** It is the most direct point-by-point claim that SAFe violates the Manifesto and Scrum’s inspect-and-adapt model.

## The best three to begin with

I would read them in this order:

1. **Ken Schwaber** — why prescribed enterprise process conflicts with Agile.
2. **Marty Cagan** — how SAFe preserves PMO structures and delivery teams.
3. **Jeff Gothelf** — how release-train planning conflicts with discovery and learning.

Together, those give you a particularly strong argument:

> SAFe standardizes practices that should remain contextual, preserves the organizational structures that create dependencies, and protects planned delivery from the discoveries that should change the plan.

Snowden then supplies the complexity-theory explanation, while Beedle supplies the direct Manifesto-based indictment.

## ChatGPT

There is no perfect one-for-one replacement because **SAFe tries to cover nearly everything**: team process, cross-team coordination, portfolio management, architecture, budgeting, governance, and organizational structure. Most alternatives deliberately cover a smaller area.

## The main direct alternatives

### 1. LeSS — Large-Scale Scrum

**Best philosophical alternative to SAFe**, especially given Ron Jeffries’ criticism.

LeSS starts with the idea that scaling should mean **simplifying and redesigning the organization**, not adding a coordination hierarchy. Multiple cross-functional feature teams work on one product, using one Product Backlog and one Product Owner. Its official material explicitly describes LeSS as organizational redesign rather than merely “scaling a framework.” citeturn643680search19turn643680search21

The contrast is approximately:

- **SAFe:** Accept dependencies and coordinate them through program structures.
- **LeSS:** Change teams and architecture to eliminate as many dependencies as possible.
- **SAFe:** Add roles between teams and leadership.
- **LeSS:** Remove layers and broaden team responsibility.
- **SAFe:** Coordinate component teams.
- **LeSS:** Move toward customer-focused feature teams.

LeSS is consequently much more disruptive. An organization cannot install it while leaving its component departments, project funding and specialist silos substantially untouched.

**Best for:** Several teams developing one genuine product.

**Major limitation:** It does not provide SAFe-style machinery for portfolio budgeting, governance and hundreds of unrelated initiatives. That omission is partly intentional.

---

### 2. Nexus

Nexus was created by Ken Schwaber as a **minimal extension of Scrum** for several teams working on one product. Teams share a Product Backlog and are jointly responsible for producing an integrated increment. Its principal concerns are integration problems, transparency and reducing cross-team dependencies. citeturn643680search9turn643680search11

Nexus adds relatively little:

- Nexus Sprint Planning;
- a Nexus Daily Scrum;
- a shared review and retrospective;
- cross-team refinement;
- a Nexus Integration Team.

It is less radical than LeSS. LeSS asks, “How must we redesign the organization?” Nexus asks, “What additional coordination is necessary when several Scrum teams build the same product?”

**Best for:** An organization already using Scrum successfully that needs several teams to collaborate on one product.

**Major limitation:** It does not solve enterprise portfolio management, budgeting or broad organizational strategy.

---

### 3. Scrum@Scale

Created by Scrum co-creator Jeff Sutherland, Scrum@Scale recursively connects Scrum teams through “Scrum of Scrums” structures. It separates two related concerns:

- coordinating how work gets delivered;
- coordinating what should be built and prioritized.

Unlike SAFe, Scrum@Scale describes itself as lightweight and adaptable rather than a turnkey, one-size-fits-all operating model. citeturn643680search2turn643680search20

It can reach more of the enterprise than Nexus because its coordination structures can be repeated at multiple levels.

The risk is that an organization can reproduce a large hierarchy of meetings and coordinating groups. It is lighter than SAFe on paper, but recursive coordination does not automatically remove the underlying dependencies.

**Best for:** Organizations committed to Scrum that need coordination beyond a single product group.

**Major limitation:** It may coordinate dependencies more than structurally eliminate them.

---

### 4. Disciplined Agile

Disciplined Agile is a broad **process-decision toolkit** rather than one prescribed lifecycle. It gathers practices from Agile, Lean and some traditional approaches and asks teams to select a context-appropriate “way of working.” PMI describes it as context-sensitive and explicitly rejects the assumption that one method fits every situation. citeturn643680search0turn643680search13

This makes it one of the closest alternatives in **breadth** to SAFe. It addresses more than team-level Scrum and includes guidance for delivery, governance and enterprise concerns.

However, it does not necessarily make the structural criticism of SAFe disappear. A company could use Disciplined Agile to improve existing processes without creating autonomous feature teams or changing funding and authority.

**Best for:** Large organizations that need broad guidance but want teams to choose among multiple lifecycles and practices.

**Major limitation:** It can become a menu of process choices rather than a force for organizational simplification.

---

### 5. Flight Levels

Flight Levels is a significantly different answer. It does not attempt to impose one development method on every team. Instead, it visualizes and improves work at three connected levels:

1. operational teams;
2. coordination across teams and services;
3. strategic portfolio direction.

Its central argument is that making individual teams Agile does not necessarily improve the overall delivery system. The organization must connect strategy to execution and optimize how work flows across teams. citeturn962531search0turn962531search5

Teams might use Scrum, Kanban, XP or something else locally. Flight Levels provides coordination above the teams without requiring a release train or standardized sprint cadence.

**Best for:** Organizations with many teams, services and products that want flow-based coordination rather than synchronized planning.

**Major limitation:** It does not itself tell you how to engineer software, design team boundaries or conduct product discovery.

---

## Other important approaches

### Team Topologies

Team Topologies is not a complete scaled-Agile framework, but it may address the **most important problem that SAFe merely coordinates**: organizational dependencies.

It defines stream-aligned, platform, enabling and complicated-subsystem teams, along with specific interaction modes. The objective is to align teams to value streams, reduce cognitive load and minimize blocking dependencies. citeturn962531search1turn962531search6turn962531search40

In practice, it complements LeSS or Flight Levels:

- LeSS provides the product-group operating structure.
- Team Topologies helps locate sensible team and system boundaries.
- XP and continuous delivery provide the engineering practices.
- Flight Levels or portfolio Kanban connects work to strategy.

This combination addresses Jeffries’ point more directly than simply choosing another coordination framework.

### Product operating model

Marty Cagan’s product operating model replaces temporary project teams and feature roadmaps with durable, empowered product teams responsible for measurable customer and business outcomes. It emphasizes discovery, empowered teams and outcomes rather than executing predetermined feature plans. citeturn962531search4turn962531search18

It is not a detailed process framework. It is an organizational and managerial model that can be combined with XP, Scrum, Kanban, Team Topologies and continuous delivery.

### FAST Agile

FAST organizes a larger group as a fluid collective whose members self-select around work rather than remaining inside rigidly assigned teams. Its official description calls it both a method and framework for self-organization at scale. citeturn962531search3turn962531search8

It is interesting and structurally bold, but much less established than LeSS, Nexus or Scrum@Scale.

### unFIX

Jurgen Appelo’s unFIX model provides modular organizational-design patterns rather than one fixed structure. It covers teaming, decision-making and organizational bases that can be combined according to context. citeturn962531search2turn962531search17

It is useful for designing an organization, but is not a complete delivery system comparable to SAFe.

## Which alternative best fits which criticism?

| Your main objection to SAFe | Strongest alternative |
|---|---|
| Too many roles, layers and ceremonies | **LeSS** |
| Several Scrum teams cannot integrate reliably | **Nexus** |
| Need Scrum across a very large organization | **Scrum@Scale** |
| One prescribed method cannot fit every context | **Disciplined Agile** |
| Team-level Agile does not improve enterprise flow | **Flight Levels** |
| Component teams create excessive dependencies | **Team Topologies** |
| Teams merely execute predetermined features | **Product operating model** |
| Permanent team boundaries are themselves limiting | **FAST** |

## My recommendation based on Jeffries’ critique

The most coherent alternative is not another enormous all-inclusive framework. It is a smaller combination:

> **LeSS or a product operating model for organizational structure, Team Topologies for dependency reduction, XP and continuous delivery for engineering, and Flight Levels or Kanban for portfolio-level flow.**

That stack changes the fundamental response to dependencies:

- SAFe makes dependencies visible and coordinates them.
- This alternative first tries to **remove the organizational, architectural and funding structures that create them**.
- The dependencies that genuinely remain are then managed visibly through flow-based coordination.

Of the named frameworks, **LeSS is the clearest ideological opposite of SAFe**. Nexus is the safest incremental alternative, while Flight Levels is probably the strongest alternative for an organization that needs portfolio coordination but does not want everyone synchronized into release trains.

## The fundamental differences

These approaches are not all competing at the same level:

| Question being answered | Frameworks most focused on it |
|---|---|
| How should one team inspect and adapt its work? | **Scrum, Crystal** |
| How should software be engineered safely and rapidly? | **XP** |
| How should work flow through a system? | **Kanban, Lean** |
| How should a governed Agile project be managed? | **DSDM / AgilePM** |
| How can several teams build one product? | **LeSS, Nexus** |
| How can Scrum structures be repeated across an organization? | **Scrum@Scale** |
| How can a large enterprise coordinate portfolios and programs? | **SAFe, Disciplined Agile** |

## Condensed assessment

- **Best lightweight management framework:** Scrum  
- **Best software-engineering method:** XP  
- **Best flow-management approach:** Kanban  
- **Best for governed Agile projects:** DSDM  
- **Best minimalist multi-team framework:** Nexus  
- **Best structural multi-team redesign:** LeSS  
- **Broadest prescriptive enterprise framework:** SAFe  
- **Broadest context-sensitive enterprise toolkit:** Disciplined Agile  
- **Best when an organization insists on scaling Scrum recursively:** Scrum@Scale  

The most important comparison is therefore not simply “Which framework is best?” It is:

> **What problem is the framework expected to solve, and which important problems does it intentionally leave outside its scope?**

Scrum appears incomplete beside SAFe because it intentionally says almost nothing about enterprise governance. SAFe appears cumbersome beside Scrum because it attempts to specify an enterprise operating model. XP looks narrow from a portfolio perspective, but it is substantially deeper than either one on the actual engineering of software.

## Story splitting as a boundary practice

Story splitting sits at the intersection of at least three perspectives:

| Participant | What they bring |
|---|---|
| **Product Owner or product person** | The problem, desired outcome, customer context, priority, and business constraints |
| **Developers** | Technical possibilities, architecture, risk, sequencing, deployability, and hidden complexity |
| **Scrum Master or facilitator** | Conversation structure, participation, flow concerns, assumptions, and awareness of oversized or blocked work |

The activity should not be:

> The Product Owner writes a large story and the developers cut it into technical tasks.

Nor should it be:

> The Scrum Master teaches developers an approved splitting formula and checks that every story is small enough.

It should be closer to:

> The group explores how a meaningful change could be delivered and learned from in smaller steps.

That conversation may reveal that the original “story” is not really a story. It may be:

- a feature containing several behaviours;
- a project milestone;
- a technical solution disguised as a requirement;
- a workflow involving multiple user decisions;
- an uncertain idea requiring discovery;
- several independently valuable outcomes bundled together.

Story splitting is therefore partly **requirements discovery**, partly **product design**, partly **technical design**, and partly **delivery planning**.

## Baseball equivalent

The closest baseball analogy is not teaching someone how to swing. It is the meeting between coaches and players where they turn a broad intention into playable decisions.

The manager may say:

> “We need to manufacture a run against this pitcher.”

That is still too broad to execute. The coaches and players must work through questions such as:

- Who is likely to reach base?
- Should the runner attempt a steal?
- Is the batter capable of bunting?
- How is the defense positioned?
- What happens if the count reaches two strikes?
- What signal will trigger the play?
- What is the fallback when the situation changes?

The resulting play is not determined by the manager alone. The player may know that the proposed bunt is unrealistic against that pitcher. The third-base coach may see a weakness in the defense. The analyst may know that stealing is a poor bet. The broad objective is progressively converted into **small executable decisions**.

Story splitting performs a similar function:

> “Let users manage restaurant reviews” becomes a series of decisions about which user, which action, which data, which path, which rules, and which observable outcome should come first.

## The major interface practices

### Story splitting

**Interface:** Product ↔ development, often facilitated by a Scrum Master.

**Purpose:** Find smaller increments that retain meaning, can be completed coherently, and ideally produce feedback.

**Common methods:**

- split by workflow step;
- split by business rule;
- split by scenario;
- split by data variation;
- split happy path from exceptions;
- split read from write behaviour;
- split by user role;
- split by operational capability;
- split by progressive fidelity;
- split by experiment or learning objective.

**Common failure:** Story splitting becomes a size-reduction exercise rather than an exploration of delivery and learning.

In baseball terms, the team stops asking how to score and starts asking only how many separate instructions can be written on the play card.

### Backlog refinement

**Interface:** Product Owner ↔ developers, with facilitation where useful.

**Purpose:** Develop shared understanding before a commitment or implementation decision.

It may include:

- clarifying the user or business problem;
- identifying assumptions;
- discussing examples;
- considering solution options;
- exposing dependencies;
- splitting work;
- identifying technical uncertainty;
- deciding whether something is ready for implementation.

**Common failure:** Refinement becomes a presentation in which the Product Owner explains completed requirements to passive developers.

A healthy refinement session changes the proposed work. If developers never challenge, reshape, combine, split, or reject anything, it is probably not collaborative refinement.

**Interface:** Product ↔ development ↔ testing or quality perspectives.

Example mapping usually explores:

- business rules;
- concrete examples;
- unanswered questions.

It helps reveal that people may agree with a sentence while imagining entirely different behaviours.

For example:

- Before or after payment?
- Before shipment?
- Who receives a refund?
- Can part of an order be cancelled?
- What happens to promotional discounts?

### Specification by example and acceptance-test-driven development

**Interface:** Product expectations ↔ executable system behaviour.

The group uses concrete examples to describe what the system should do. Those examples may later become automated tests.

This is more than testing. It creates a bridge between:

- business language;
- development decisions;
- verification;
- documentation.

**Common failure:** Developers receive prewritten acceptance tests as contractual instructions. The useful part is the conversation that produces the examples, not merely encoding them afterward.

### User-story mapping

**Interface:** Product strategy ↔ workflow design ↔ release planning.

Story mapping lays out the user’s journey and arranges possible increments beneath it. It helps the group see:

- the complete workflow;
- alternative paths;
- missing steps;
- possible release slices;
- where early value or learning may be obtained.

Its baseball counterpart is mapping the whole offensive sequence rather than discussing one isolated at-bat.

**Common failure:** It becomes another large requirements inventory, merely arranged horizontally.

---

### Impact mapping

**Interface:** Organizational goal ↔ customer behaviour ↔ possible product changes.

It works backward from:

1. the goal;
2. the actors who can affect it;
3. the behavioural impacts needed;
4. possible deliverables.

This prevents the team from immediately treating a requested feature as the objective.

In baseball:

- Goal: score more runs.
- Actor: opposing pitcher.
- Desired impact: force him to throw more pitches.
- Possible tactics: patient at-bats, selective aggression, lineup changes.

The feature equivalent should remain one possible intervention, not the unquestioned destination.

### Estimation

**Interface:** Product decisions ↔ development uncertainty.

Estimation can help expose:

- misunderstood scope;
- hidden technical work;
- dependencies;
- uncertainty;
- disagreements about implementation;
- assumptions about quality.

Its greatest value is frequently the conversation rather than the resulting number.

**Common failure:** The number becomes a promise, performance measure, or accounting unit. The interface then turns adversarial: product wants a smaller number and developers defend a larger one.

The baseball equivalent would be asking coaches how many runs a proposed strategy will produce and later judging them by whether the estimate was exact.

---

### Sprint planning or replenishment

**Interface:** Priority ↔ available capacity ↔ current system conditions.

The group decides what should enter the active workflow. This involves more than selecting the top backlog items:

- Is the objective coherent?
- Is necessary knowledge available?
- Is the work independent enough to begin?
- Are there operational obligations?
- Is unfinished work already consuming capacity?
- Does the selected work form a sensible goal?

**Common failure:** It becomes a loading exercise in which management attempts to fill every apparent hour.

---

### Definition of Done

**Interface:** Development ↔ product expectations ↔ organizational risk.

The Definition of Done creates a shared agreement about what “completed” means:

- coded;
- reviewed;
- tested;
- integrated;
- secured;
- documented;
- deployed or deployable;
- observable in production.

It prevents each role from silently using a different definition of completion.

The baseball equivalent is agreeing that a play is not complete merely because the ball was hit. The runner must safely reach the intended base, the score must be recorded, and no rule violation can invalidate it.

---

### Retrospective

**Interface:** Team experience ↔ process change.

The retrospective turns observations about the work into experiments for changing the system.

It can address:

- coordination;
- quality;
- decision-making;
- workload;
- tooling;
- interruptions;
- relationships;
- recurring blockers.

**Common failure:** The Scrum Master owns the retrospective while developers and product participants contribute complaints but have little authority to alter the conditions creating them.

A coach reviewing game footage has little value if the organization refuses to change the lineup, training, tactics, or decision rights.

## Who should “own” story splitting?

No single role should own the substance.

The **Product Owner** should not unilaterally decide the slices because they may not understand technical feasibility or deployability.

The **developers** should not unilaterally decide because they may decompose the work into technical layers that produce no observable outcome.

The **Scrum Master** may teach splitting approaches and facilitate the discussion, but should not become the story-splitting authority.

A better division is:

- **Product owns the reason and ordering decisions.**
- **Developers own technical integrity and implementation decisions.**
- **The team jointly discovers useful increments.**
- **A facilitator improves the quality of the conversation.**

This is why story splitting feels like an interface. Its value comes from forcing two incomplete models to meet:

> Product knows why the change might matter but cannot completely know how it should be constructed. Developers understand the construction but cannot independently determine which compromises retain product value.

The split emerges from their interaction.

## Where the Agile roles fit

### Product Owner

The Product Owner resembles neither the owner of the baseball club nor the field manager exactly. The closest useful analogy is someone responsible for deciding:

- which games or outcomes matter;
- which opportunities deserve attention;
- which trade-offs are acceptable;
- what should be attempted first.

The Product Owner should strongly influence game planning but should not dictate batting mechanics.

Likewise, the Product Owner can prioritize a user outcome but should not prescribe the internal technical decomposition without developer involvement.

### Developers

Developers resemble players, but they also resemble coaches because software developers design the system while constructing it.

- discover technical constraints;
- change the implementation plan;
- influence scope;
- design architecture;
- automate future execution;
- identify alternative product possibilities.

### Scrum Master

- facilitator;
- coordination coach;
- process observer;

- introduce a splitting pattern;
- challenge whether the proposed pieces produce meaningful feedback;
- stop the group from turning the conversation into task assignment.

- developing player capability;
- maintaining a sustainable roster;
- resolving structural constraints;
- improving technical decision-making;
- helping the team develop a coherent playing style.

A technical lead may have significant influence over implementation strategy, but should not automatically own product priority.

A useful baseball formulation is:

> The front office chooses what kind of club to build. The manager and coaches prepare a plan. The players help determine whether that plan can work and make the decisions that can only be made on the field.

The software version is:

> Leadership defines strategic constraints. Product and developers jointly shape the work. Specialists contribute their perspective. Facilitators improve the conversation. Developers own implementation and adapt as reality emerges.

Problems begin when one level reaches too far into another:

- Executives prescribe coding practices.
- Product Owners prescribe technical tasks.
- Developers determine product value without product or customer input.
- Scrum Masters become backlog police.

- Teams make local decisions that conflict with organizational strategy.

This distinguishes approaches that may otherwise look similar:

- Kanban generally starts with the existing process and evolves it.
- LeSS usually requires significant organizational redesign.
- SAFe is frequently introduced through training, defined configurations, and planned implementation.
- XP practices can often be adopted incrementally, although the practices reinforce one another.

I would keep this separate from the core ten because it describes **how the approach is introduced**, not how the resulting system operates.

The strongest overall model is therefore:

> **Objective, scope, unit of work, intervention type, decision horizon, decision rights, coordination, prescription, feedback, and context—plus adoption strategy when organizational change is being compared.**

1. **What kind of change would address this?**
2. **Where and for how long should we try it?**

- situational tactics;

| Output | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Team experiment** | We have authority to try this ourselves |

Ask:

> Is this a problem with technique, tactics, the game plan, team design, or organizational strategy?

### 2. Match the intervention to the cause

- a technique problem with a reorganization;
- a structural problem with “communicate better”;
- a strategy problem with another ceremony;
- a situational problem with a permanent policy.

Ask:

> Can we try this ourselves, do we need to collaborate with someone, or must someone else make the decision?

> For the next two weeks, we will limit active stories to two and swarm when the limit is reached. We will review blocked time and carry-over.

> For the next two integrations, we will involve the platform team during refinement. Engineering leadership will also review whether deployment ownership should move to the product teams.

> **Retrospectives may discover improvements at every level. Teams should directly experiment at the levels they control, collaborate where responsibilities are shared, and escalate structural changes rather than disguising them as local process improvements.**

So the five levels are not five categories from which every retro must select an item. They are a **diagnostic ladder** that prevents the team from treating every failure as a problem with how individual players performed.

## A useful test

Ask:

> **Is this a repeatable way of performing a task or skill?**

Then it is probably a technique.

- **TDD** is a technique.
- **Pair programming** is a technique.
- **Story splitting** is a collaborative planning technique.
- **“Pair whenever a story is high risk”** is a tactic.
- **“All work passes through a specialist review team”** is a role or workflow design.
- **Scrum, XP, and Kanban** are broader team philosophies or methods composed of multiple techniques, policies, and feedback mechanisms.
